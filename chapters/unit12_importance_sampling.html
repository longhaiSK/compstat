Rejection sampling turns draws from an envelope \(g \ge f\) into exact draws from \(f\), but every rejected proposal is wasted, and in the tails of a poor envelope almost every proposal is rejected. Importance sampling keeps every proposal instead of discarding most of them: draw from an easier function \(g\) and attach a weight to each draw that corrects for sampling from the wrong distribution. The price is that the resulting sample is no longer an exact iid sample from \(f\) — it is a weighted sample — but nothing is ever thrown away, and no envelope condition \(g \ge f\) is required.

12.1 The Reweighting Idea

12.1.1 The goal

As in the last two chapters, \(f(\theta)\) is an unnormalized function of interest — typically \(f(\theta) = L(\theta)\pi(\theta)\) for a likelihood \(L\) and prior \(\pi\) — and we want two kinds of integral: \[ A = E_f\big[a(\theta)\big] = \frac{\int a(\theta)\,f(\theta)\,d\theta}{\int f(\theta)\,d\theta}, \qquad\qquad C_f = \int f(\theta)\,d\theta, \] a posterior expectation and a normalizing constant (the marginal likelihood when \(f = L\pi\)). Plain Monte Carlo needs draws from \(f/C_f\), which we generally cannot produce.

Suppose instead we can draw from another function \(g(\theta)\) (also possibly unnormalized, with \(C_g = \int g(\theta)\,d\theta\)). Wherever \(f\) puts mass, \(g\) must too: \[ \{\theta : g(\theta) > 0\} \ \supseteq\ \{\theta : f(\theta) > 0\}. \] Unlike rejection sampling, \(g\) need not dominate \(f\) everywhere — there is no requirement \(g \ge f\). This is what makes importance sampling applicable in situations where no usable envelope exists.

12.1.2 Two estimators

Define the importance weight \(w(\theta) = f(\theta)/g(\theta)\). For any \(a\), \[ E_f\big[a(\theta)\big] = \int a(\theta)\,\frac{f(\theta)}{C_f}\,d\theta = \frac{C_g}{C_f}\, E_g\big[a(\theta)\,w(\theta)\big], \] and taking \(a \equiv 1\) gives \(C_f/C_g = E_g[w(\theta)]\). Combining the two, \[ E_f\big[a(\theta)\big] = \frac{E_g\big[a(\theta)\,w(\theta)\big]}{E_g\big[w(\theta)\big]}. \] Draw \(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_S \overset{iid}{\sim} g/C_g\) and compute \(w_s = w(\theta_s)\). Two estimators follow directly:

  • Ratio of normalizing constants: \(\widehat{C_f/C_g} = \frac1S\sum_s w_s\), unbiased for \(C_f/C_g\); if \(g\) is already a normalized density (\(C_g=1\)) this estimates \(C_f\) itself.
  • Self-normalized expectation: \(\hat A = \sum_{s=1}^S a(\theta_s)\,\tilde w_s\), with normalized weights \(\tilde w_s = w_s / \sum_j w_j\) summing to \(1\). This turns the proposal sample into a weighted sample from \(f\): any unknown constants in \(f\) and \(g\) cancel in the ratio, so \(\hat A\) needs only \(f\) and \(g\) up to a proportionality constant. It is a ratio of two averages and so is biased with bias \(O(1/S)\), but consistent.

12.1.3 Effective sample size

\(\hat A\) is a weighted average of \(a(\theta_s)\): draws where \(f \gg g\) count more, draws where \(f \ll g\) count less. How many equally weighted draws is the weighted sample worth? \[ S_{\text{eff}} = \frac{1}{\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^S \tilde w_s^2}. \] If the weights are all equal, \(\tilde w_s = 1/S\) and \(S_{\text{eff}} = 1/(S \cdot 1/S^2) = S\): the proposal is shaped just like the target, and every draw counts fully. If one weight dominates all the others, \(\tilde w_s \approx 1\) for that draw and \(S_{\text{eff}} \approx 1\): only one draw is doing any work. A variance heuristic makes this precise: \(\text{Var}_g(\hat A) \approx \text{Var}_f(a)/S_{\text{eff}}\), the same form as for an iid sample of size \(S_{\text{eff}}\) from \(f\) itself.

The catch, central to this chapter, is that \(S_{\text{eff}}\) is computed only from the weights actually observed. A proposal that never visits the part of \(\theta\)-space where \(f\) and \(g\) disagree most will report a reassuring \(S_{\text{eff}}\) while being badly wrong — this is exactly the subject of the “bad stories” below.

12.2 Example 1: Tail Probability of Cauchy

Write the proposal on the standardized scale \(z=(x-\mu)/\sigma\) with standardized cutoff \(a=(c-\mu)/\sigma\), so that \[ h(z) \;=\; \log\varphi(z) \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{2}z^{2} + \text{const}, \qquad h'(z) = -z , \qquad h''(z) = -1 < 0 . \] Restricting \(z\) to \((a,\infty)\) intersects the support with a convex set and therefore preserves concavity of \(h\). A single sampler on the \(z\) scale consequently serves every parameter combination: only \(a\) changes, and draws are returned as \(x=\mu+\sigma z\).

ARS exploits concavity twice. Tangent lines to \(h\) at abscissae \(x_{1}<\dots<x_{k}\) in \((a,\infty)\) lie above \(h\), so their pointwise minimum is an upper hull \(u(z)\ge h(z)\); exponentiating gives a piecewise-exponential envelope that is trivial to sample by inversion within each piece. Chords joining adjacent \((x_{j},h(x_{j}))\) lie below \(h\), so they supply a lower hull \(\ell(z)\le h(z)\), the squeeze. Each candidate \(z^{*}\) drawn from the envelope is accepted at once if \(\log U \le \ell(z^{*})-u(z^{*})\), and only otherwise is \(h(z^{*})\) evaluated; when it is evaluated, \(z^{*}\) is appended to the abscissae and the hull is rebuilt, so the envelope tightens as sampling proceeds. Two structural requirements are worth noting. The piece boundaries are the tangent intersections \[ z_{j} \;=\; \frac{h(x_{j+1}) - h(x_{j}) - x_{j+1}h'(x_{j+1}) + x_{j}h'(x_{j})} {h'(x_{j}) - h'(x_{j+1})} , \] which for the Gaussian collapse to the midpoints \((x_{j}+x_{j+1})/2\); and the envelope is integrable on the unbounded right piece only if \(h'(x_{k})<0\), so the largest abscissa must exceed the mode. The lower end of the domain is the finite cutoff \(a\), where no such condition is needed.

The implementation below adapts until the hull reaches max_hull points and then freezes it, after which the remaining draws are generated by ordinary vectorized rejection from a fixed envelope. Roughly two dozen tangents give acceptance rates above \(0.95\) across the whole parameter range used here.

Code 
## ---------------------------------------------------------------------
##  rtnorm_ars(): adaptive rejection sampling from
##      g(x)  proportional to  phi((x - mu) / sigma) * I(x > c)
##  Returns a numeric vector with attributes "hull" (final abscissae, on
##  the original scale) and "acc_rate" (accepted draws per envelope draw).
## ---------------------------------------------------------------------
rtnorm_ars <- function(n, mu = 0, sigma = 1, c = 0,
                       max_hull = 25, batch = 4096) {

    stopifnot(n >= 1, sigma > 0, is.finite(c), max_hull >= 3)

    a  <- (c - mu) / sigma                 # standardized cutoff
    h  <- function(z) -z^2 / 2             # log density, up to a constant
    hp <- function(z) -z                   # its derivative

    ## ---- upper hull: tangents at the abscissae x ---------------------
    ## Piece j is the tangent at x[j], valid on [z[j], z[j+1]], where the
    ## z[] are the tangent intersections, z[1] = a and z[k+1] = Inf.
    build <- function(x) {
        x <- sort(unique(x))
        x[x == 0] <- 1e-8                  # keep every slope non-zero
        x <- sort(unique(x))
        k  <- length(x)
        hx <- h(x)
        sx <- hp(x)

        j  <- seq_len(k - 1)
        ## general ARS intersection formula; for the Gaussian it reduces
        ## to the midpoint (x[j] + x[j+1]) / 2
        zz <- (hx[j + 1] - hx[j] - x[j + 1] * sx[j + 1] + x[j] * sx[j]) /
              (sx[j] - sx[j + 1])
        z  <- c(a, zz, Inf)

        ul <- hx + sx * (z[1:k] - x)       # hull at the left end of piece j
        ur <- hx + sx * (z[2:(k + 1)] - x) # hull at the right end (-Inf on the last)

        ## log mass of each piece, |exp(ul) - exp(ur)| / |slope|, computed
        ## with a per-piece offset so that nothing under- or overflows
        M  <- pmax(ul, ur)
        lp <- M + log(abs(exp(ul - M) - exp(ur - M))) - log(abs(sx))
        lp[!is.finite(lp)] <- -Inf

        list(x = x, hx = hx, sx = sx, z = z, ul = ul, ur = ur, M = M,
             p = exp(lp - max(lp)))
    }

    ## ---- draw m points from the piecewise-exponential envelope -------
    draw <- function(H, m) {
        j  <- sample.int(length(H$x), m, replace = TRUE, prob = H$p)
        v  <- runif(m)
        Mj <- H$M[j]
        el <- exp(H$ul[j] - Mj)
        er <- exp(H$ur[j] - Mj)
        lu <- Mj + log(el + v * (er - el))      # inverse cdf within the piece
        list(z = H$x[j] + (lu - H$hx[j]) / H$sx[j], u = lu)
    }

    ## ---- lower hull (squeeze): chords between adjacent abscissae -----
    squeeze <- function(H, zs) {
        k   <- length(H$x)
        i   <- findInterval(zs, H$x)
        out <- rep(-Inf, length(zs))
        ok  <- i >= 1 & i <= k - 1
        if (any(ok)) {
            ii <- i[ok]; zz <- zs[ok]
            out[ok] <- ((H$x[ii + 1] - zz) * H$hx[ii] +
                        (zz - H$x[ii]) * H$hx[ii + 1]) /
                       (H$x[ii + 1] - H$x[ii])
        }
        out
    }

    ## ---- initial abscissae -------------------------------------------
    ## All must lie in (a, Inf) and the largest must have a negative slope,
    ## so that the final piece of the envelope is integrable.  The local
    ## scale 1 / max(|a|, 1) reflects the near-exponential behaviour of the
    ## Gaussian tail just above a large cutoff.
    s0 <- 1 / max(abs(a), 1)
    H  <- build(c(a + 1e-3 * s0, a + 0.5 * s0, a + 2 * s0, max(a, 0) + 1.5))

    out <- numeric(n); got <- 0L; tries <- 0L

    ## ---- phase 1: adaptive, one draw at a time ------------------------
    while (got < n && length(H$x) < max_hull) {
        d  <- draw(H, 1); tries <- tries + 1L
        lw <- log(runif(1))
        if (lw <= squeeze(H, d$z) - d$u) {          # cheap squeeze test
            got <- got + 1L; out[got] <- d$z
        } else {                                    # full test, then adapt
            if (lw <= h(d$z) - d$u) { got <- got + 1L; out[got] <- d$z }
            H <- build(c(H$x, d$z))
        }
    }

    ## ---- phase 2: envelope frozen, vectorized rejection ---------------
    while (got < n) {
        m    <- max(batch, n - got)
        d    <- draw(H, m); tries <- tries + m
        zs   <- d$z[log(runif(m)) <= h(d$z) - d$u]
        take <- min(length(zs), n - got)
        if (take > 0L) out[got + seq_len(take)] <- zs[seq_len(take)]
        got <- got + take
    }

    structure(mu + sigma * out,
              hull     = mu + sigma * H$x,
              acc_rate = n / tries)
}

The contrast with the target is the point of the exercise. ARS requires log-concavity, and the standard Cauchy is not log-concave: \[ \bigl(\log f_{\text{Cauchy}}\bigr)''(x) \;=\; \frac{2(x^{2}-1)}{(1+x^{2})^{2}} , \] which is positive for \(|x|>1\), so \(\log f_{\text{Cauchy}}\) is convex in both tails and no tangent line lies above it there. The hull construction fails outright. This is not an incidental obstacle: log-concavity forces tails to decay at least exponentially, which is precisely the property that disqualifies the normal as a proposal for a Cauchy target. The densities ARS handles most comfortably are, tail for tail, the ones least able to serve as importance proposals for heavy-tailed targets.

12.2.1 The estimator and its variance

With \(g\) the normalized truncated normal on \((c,\infty)\) and \(f(x)=f_{\text{Cauchy}}(x)I(x>c)\), the weights are \(w(x)=f(x)/g(x)\) and \[ \widehat{C} \;=\; \frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S} w(X_{i}), \qquad X_{i}\sim g , \] is unbiased for \(C_{f}=P(X>c)\) for every \((\mu,\sigma)\). Its variance is \[ \operatorname{Var}_{g}\bigl(w(X)\bigr) \;=\; \int_{c}^{\infty}\frac{f_{\text{Cauchy}}(x)^{2}}{g(x)}\,dx \;-\; C_{f}^{2} , \] and since \(f_{\text{Cauchy}}(x)^{2}\asymp \pi^{-2}x^{-4}\) while \(g(x)\propto\exp\{-(x-\mu)^{2}/2\sigma^{2}\}\), the integrand grows like \(x^{-4}\exp\{(x-\mu)^{2}/2\sigma^{2}\}\). The integral diverges for every finite \(\mu\) and every \(\sigma>0\). No choice of proposal parameters repairs the estimator; the parameters govern only how quickly the damage becomes visible. Reported standard errors and confidence intervals are estimating a quantity that does not exist, and the sample standard deviation of the weights, being finite by construction, gives no warning of this.

12.2.2 An interactive view

The app below puts \(c\), \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) under slider control and displays, on a common \(x\)-axis: the two normalized truncated densities on a log scale with a rug of the draws, and the normalized importance weights \(\tilde w_{i}=w_{i}/\sum_{j}w_{j}\) at the sampled points. Because both densities are normalized, the vertical gap between the curves on the log scale is \(\log w(x)-\log C_{f}\), so the visual divergence of the curves is the growth of the weight function. The region beyond the largest draw is shaded: the proposal’s shape there is never interrogated by the sample, and it is exactly there that the target carries the mass the estimator is missing. The legends report the true \(P(X>c)\), the estimate with its nominal 95% interval, and \(S_{\text{eff}}=1/\sum_{i}\tilde w_{i}^{2}\).

server <- function(input, output) {

    sim <- reactive({
        S  <- as.integer(input$S)
        cc <- input$cc; mu <- input$mu; sg <- input$sg
        set.seed(input$seed + 1000 * input$new)

        X  <- rtnorm_ars(S, mu, sg, cc)

        ## log importance weight  log f_Cauchy(x) - log g(x),  with g the
        ## normalized truncated normal on (c, Inf)
        lZ   <- pnorm(cc, mu, sg, lower.tail = FALSE, log.p = TRUE)
        logw <- dcauchy(X, log = TRUE) - (dnorm(X, mu, sg, log = TRUE) - lZ)

        m    <- max(logw)
        v    <- exp(logw - m)                       # rescaled weights
        Chat <- mean(v) * exp(m)                    # unbiased estimate of P(X > c)
        se   <- sd(v) / sqrt(S) * exp(m)            # nominal standard error
        wn   <- v / sum(v)                          # normalized weights

        list(X = X, wn = wn, S = S, cc = cc, mu = mu, sg = sg,
             Chat = Chat, se = se, ess = 1 / sum(wn^2),
             truth = pcauchy(cc, lower.tail = FALSE),
             acc = attr(X, "acc_rate"))
    })

    output$panels <- renderPlot({
        s  <- sim()
        cc <- s$cc; X <- s$X
        lo <- cc
        hi <- max(max(X) + 0.5, cc + 5)
        xs <- seq(lo, hi, length.out = 800)

        ## both densities normalized on (c, Inf)
        ft <- dcauchy(xs) / s$truth
        gt <- dnorm(xs, s$mu, s$sg) / pnorm(cc, s$mu, s$sg, lower.tail = FALSE)
        yl <- range(c(ft, gt)[c(ft, gt) > 0])
        yl[1] <- max(yl[1], 1e-12)

        lwr <- s$Chat - 1.96 * s$se
        upr <- s$Chat + 1.96 * s$se
        covered <- lwr <= s$truth && s$truth <= upr

        layout(matrix(1:2, 2), heights = c(3, 2))

        ## ---- top panel: the two densities ----------------------------
        par(mar = c(0.4, 5, 3, 1))
        plot(xs, ft, type = "n", log = "y", ylim = yl, xlim = c(lo, hi),
             xaxt = "n", xlab = "", ylab = "density (log scale)",
             main = "target and proposal, both normalized on (c, Inf)")
        rect(max(X), yl[1], hi, yl[2] * 10,
             col = adjustcolor("grey60", 0.18), border = NA)
        lines(xs, ft, lwd = 4, col = "firebrick")
        lines(xs, gt, lwd = 3, col = "steelblue")
        rug(X, col = adjustcolor("steelblue", 0.25))
        legend("topright", bty = "n", cex = 1.05,
               lwd = c(4, 3, NA, NA, NA),
               col = c("firebrick", "steelblue", NA, NA, NA),
               legend = c(
                   "target: Cauchy, truncated at c",
                   sprintf("proposal: N(%.2f, %.2f^2), truncated at c", s$mu, s$sg),
                   sprintf("true  P(X > c) = %.5g", s$truth),
                   sprintf("est.  P(X > c) = %.5g", s$Chat),
                   sprintf("95%% CI = (%.5g, %.5g)%s", lwr, upr,
                           if (covered) "" else "   << misses the truth")))
        text(max(X), yl[2], "  no draw ever reached here", adj = c(0, 1.4),
             col = "grey35", cex = 0.95)

        ## ---- bottom panel: normalized weights ------------------------
        par(mar = c(4.5, 5, 0.4, 1))
        wn <- s$wn
        if (input$logw) {

            plot(X, wn, type = "n", log = "y", ylim = wl, xlim = c(lo, hi),

Several settings repay attention.

  • Small \(\sigma\), or \(\mu\) far below \(c\). The draws pile up just above the cutoff, the weights are nearly equal among them, and \(S_{\text{eff}}\) is close to \(S\) — while the estimate sits far below the truth and the interval excludes it. This is the diagnostic failing in its most misleading form: uniform weights certify that the proposal is internally consistent, not that it has found the target’s mass.
  • Large \(\sigma\). Draws occasionally reach past the crossing of the two curves, one weight then dominates the sum, \(S_{\text{eff}}\) collapses toward \(1\), and the estimate jumps. The estimator is now visibly unreliable, which is an improvement on being invisibly unreliable.
  • Repeated resampling at fixed \(S\). Pressing New sample varies the estimate far more than the reported standard error admits, and the nominal 95% interval misses the truth far more often than 5% of the time.
  • Raising \(S\) a hundredfold. The nominal standard error falls by a factor of ten, the actual spread across resamples does not, and \(S_{\text{eff}}\) as a percentage of \(S\) deteriorates rather than improving.
  • Raising \(c\). The mismatch worsens monotonically, since the polynomial and Gaussian tails separate faster the further out one starts.

12.3 Example 2: Marginal Likelihood via Importance Sampling

12.3.1 Setup

The quadrature and Laplace-approximation chapters compute the marginal likelihood \(P(y) = \int L(\theta)\pi(\theta)\,d\theta\) of the same conjugate-free Gaussian model — unknown mean \(\mu\) and \(w=\log\sigma^2\), independent normal priors — by a product grid and by a mode-and-Hessian approximation, respectively. Importance sampling offers a third route, and one that comes with a built-in accuracy diagnostic (\(S_{\text{eff}}\)) that neither of the other two methods provides directly.

Take \(g(\mu,w)\) to be the Gaussian approximation to the posterior obtained by a Laplace fit: locate the posterior mode \((\hat\mu,\hat w)\) by optimization, take \(g = N_2\big((\hat\mu,\hat w),\, H^{-1}\big)\) for \(H\) the Hessian of \(-\log[L\pi]\) at the mode, and importance-sample from \(g\) with \(f = L\pi\): \[ \widehat{P(y)} = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^S w_s, \qquad w_s = \frac{L(\theta_s)\pi(\theta_s)}{g(\theta_s)}, \qquad \theta_s \sim g. \] Because \(g\) is shaped like the posterior near its mode, this is a “good story” proposal by construction — in sharp contrast to sampling directly from the (typically much wider, uninformative) prior, which is exactly the log_marlik_mc function used elsewhere in this book as a naive-Monte-Carlo check: it is importance sampling with \(g = \pi\), and its weights are just the likelihood values \(L(\theta_s)\).

12.3.2 An efficient implementation

Code 
log_lik   <- function(x, mu, w) sum(dnorm(x, mu, exp(w / 2), log = TRUE))
log_prior <- function(mu, w, mu_0, sigma_mu, w_0, sigma_w) {
    dnorm(mu, mu_0, sigma_mu, log = TRUE) + dnorm(w, w_0, sigma_w, log = TRUE)
}
neg_log_post <- function(theta, x, mu_0, sigma_mu, w_0, sigma_w) {
    -log_lik(x, theta[1], theta[2]) -
        log_prior(theta[1], theta[2], mu_0, sigma_mu, w_0, sigma_w)
}

### log density of N(mu, A^{-1}) at every column of a 2 x S matrix of points at once,
### replacing a per-point apply() call with one vectorized quadratic form
log_dmvnorm_batch <- function(Theta, mu, A) {
    Tc <- Theta - mu
    quad <- colSums((A %*% Tc) * Tc)
    0.5 * (-nrow(Theta) * log(2 * pi) + sum(log(svd(A)$d)) - quad)
}

### log marginal likelihood by importance sampling from a Laplace-shaped Gaussian
log_marlik_imps <- function(x, mu_0, sigma_mu, w_0, sigma_w, S) {
    fit <- nlm(neg_log_post, c(mean(x), log(stats::var(x))), hessian = TRUE,
               x = x, mu_0 = mu_0, sigma_mu = sigma_mu, w_0 = w_0, sigma_w = sigma_w)
    mu_hat <- fit$estimate; H <- fit$hessian
    L <- t(chol(solve(H)))                        # Sigma = H^{-1} = L L'
    Theta <- L %*% matrix(rnorm(2 * S), 2, S) + mu_hat

    log_g <- log_dmvnorm_batch(Theta, mu_hat, H)
    log_f <- -apply(Theta, 2, neg_log_post, x = x, mu_0 = mu_0, sigma_mu = sigma_mu,
                     w_0 = w_0, sigma_w = sigma_w)
    lw <- log_f - log_g
    structure(log_sum_exp(lw) - log(S), ess = log_ess(lw))
}

### log marginal likelihood by naive Monte Carlo from the prior (g = pi): a
### second, independent importance-sampling estimator with an unfavourable g
log_marlik_prior <- function(x, mu_0, sigma_mu, w_0, sigma_w, S) {
    mus <- rnorm(S, mu_0, sigma_mu); ws <- rnorm(S, w_0, sigma_w)
    lw <- vapply(seq_len(S), function(i) log_lik(x, mus[i], ws[i]), numeric(1))
    structure(log_sum_exp(lw) - log(S), ess = log_ess(lw))
}

Replacing the two apply() calls used in earlier drafts of this code — one for the proposal log-density, one for the log-posterior — with a single vectorized quadratic form (log_dmvnorm_batch) removes \(S\) separate R function calls for the proposal evaluation; only the data-dependent log-likelihood still needs one call per draw, since it sums over the \(n\) observations for each \(\theta_s\) separately.

12.3.3 Checking accuracy

Code 
set.seed(4)
x_small <- rnorm(10);  x_large <- rnorm(200)

check_tbl <- do.call(rbind, lapply(list(n10 = x_small, n200 = x_large), function(x) {
    reps <- replicate(500, log_marlik_imps(x, 0, 5, 0, 5, 200), simplify = FALSE)
    vals <- vapply(reps, as.numeric, numeric(1))
    esss <- vapply(reps, attr, numeric(1), which = "ess")
    data.frame(mean_log_ml = mean(vals), sd_log_ml = sd(vals), mean_ess = mean(esss))
}))
check_tbl <- cbind(dataset = c("n = 10", "n = 200"), check_tbl)
knitr::kable(check_tbl, digits = 4)

The Monte Carlo standard deviation across replications is small relative to the estimate itself, and the effective sample size stays close to the nominal \(S=200\) in both cases — evidence that the Laplace-shaped proposal tracks the posterior well even though it is, after all, only a second-order approximation to it.

12.3.4 Why the proposal matters: Laplace-shaped versus prior sampling

The prior is a valid proposal for the same target — every posterior draw is also a prior draw with positive density — so log_marlik_prior is a legitimate importance-sampling estimator too. But the prior is far more diffuse than the posterior, especially once \(n\) is not tiny, so it is a much poorer match to \(f = L\pi\).

Code 
set.seed(5)
ns <- c(5, 20, 100, 400)
ess_tbl <- do.call(rbind, lapply(ns, function(n) {
    x <- rnorm(n)
    data.frame(n = n,
               ess_laplace_proposal = attr(log_marlik_imps(x, 0, 5, 0, 5, 500), "ess"),
               ess_prior_sampling   = attr(log_marlik_prior(x, 0, 5, 0, 5, 500), "ess"))
}))
knitr::kable(ess_tbl, digits = c(0, 1, 1))

As \(n\) grows the posterior sharpens around the truth while the prior does not, so prior sampling’s effective sample size deteriorates steadily; the Laplace-shaped proposal, which re-centers and re-scales itself around the posterior mode for every data set, keeps pace. This is the marginal-likelihood instance of the same lesson as the missed-mode example above: a fixed, data-independent proposal becomes an increasingly bad match to a target that concentrates as more data arrive, and only a proposal that adapts to the target avoids the resulting collapse in efficiency.

12.4 Summary

  • Importance sampling reweights draws from a proposal \(g\) by \(w(\theta) = f(\theta)/g(\theta)\) instead of accepting or rejecting them, using \(\widehat{C_f/C_g} = \frac1S\sum_s w_s\) and the self-normalized \(\hat A = \sum_s a(\theta_s)\tilde w_s\); only the support condition \(\{g>0\}\supseteq\{f>0\}\) is required, not an envelope \(g \ge f\).
  • A good proposal shaped like the target on its support gives near-constant weights and \(S_{\text{eff}} \approx S\) (the truncated-normal and Laplace-proposal examples), and a single weighted sample can answer several questions about the same target — a probability and an expectation alike.
  • A proposal that misses a mode or part of the support fails silently: the observed weights can look perfectly healthy while both \(C_f/C_g\) and \(E_f[a]\) are badly wrong, and no amount of extra sampling fixes it. \(S_{\text{eff}}\) diagnoses only the noisy failure (wildly varying observed weights), never the silent one.
  • For the marginal likelihood, a proposal built from a Laplace approximation at the posterior mode remains efficient as the data accumulate and the posterior sharpens, while a fixed, data-independent proposal such as the prior does not — reinforcing why the mode-finding methods from earlier chapters remain useful even once we turn to sampling-based methods.
