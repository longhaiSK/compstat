A consolidated index of every numbered figure and table in the book, plus the interactive Shiny apps. Click any entry to jump to it in context.

Interactive apps

  • What goes wrong (in Optimization for Maximum Likelihood Estimation) — The interactive figure below lets you choose a starting value and step through the iterations.
  • Interactive demonstration (in EM Algorithms) — The figure below replaces the frame-by-frame animation of earlier versions of this chapter.
  • Explore it interactively (in Introduction to Bayesian Inference) — The app below lets you adjust the prior, sample size, and observed data for any of the three conjugate models and watch prior and posterior move together, along with the posterior summary table computed above.
  • When the grid misses the posterior’s mode (in Numerical Quadrature) — The app models a single unknown mean \(\mu\).
  • Adaptive Gauss–Hermite Quadrature (in Laplace Approximation) — Because \(u_i\) is uniform in Laplace-normalized space, the grid automatically concentrates where the true posterior mass lives.
  • Trying it interactively: tight vs. loose envelopes (in Rejection Sampling) — The app below scales the envelope by a factor \(c \ge 1\), \(g_c(x) = c\, e^{1/2-|x|}\): larger \(c\) keeps the envelope valid but wastes more draws (acceptance rate \(= 0.760/c\)).
  • Trying it interactively: the Cauchy envelope (in Rejection Sampling) — The app below shows the target \(f(x)=\text{Gamma}(\alpha,1)\) density against the Cauchy-based envelope \(g(x)=\exp(\texttt{log\_g\_gamma}(x,\alpha))\) as \(\alpha\) is dragged (left), and a histogram of 2000 fresh rejection-sampled draws against the true density (right) — an exact match whenever the envelope is valid.
  • Animation for Illustrating ARS (in Rejection Sampling) — The app below reimplements the same idea directly — start with a few tangent lines to \(\log f\), sample from the piecewise-exponential envelope they define, and whenever a candidate is rejected, add it as a new tangent point and rebuild the hull — one candidate at a time, so every step of the mechanism is visible. Next advances a single candidate; Play advances automatically.
  • Explore it interactively (in Importance Sampling) — The app below reproduces the missed-mode example with an adjustable proposal: move its mean and spread and watch the effective sample size stay high while the estimated mean drifts away from the true value of \(0\) whenever the proposal fails to reach both components of the target.
  • Explore it interactively (in Markov Chain Monte Carlo) — The app below runs the hmc() function above on the same correlated target and lets you vary \(\epsilon\) and \(L\) directly.

Introduction to Bayesian Inference

  • Figure 8.1 — Left: a flat-ish prior \(\pi(\theta)\) (dashed) and a likelihood \(L(\theta)\) (solid) that is much more concentrated — the data are far more informative than the prior here. Right: the posterior \(\propto L(\theta)\pi(\theta)\), essentially the (renormalized) likelihood since the prior is so flat over the region where \(L\) has mass. Shaded tails cut off \(\alpha/2\) of the posterior probability each, giving a \((1-\alpha)\) credible interval \((\theta_{\alpha/2},\theta_{1-\alpha/2})\).
  • Figure 8.2 — Red: the sampling density \(P(y_{n+1}\mid\theta_j)\) for ten posterior draws \(\theta_j\sim N(3, 0.8^2)\). Black: their average, the posterior predictive density \(P(y_{n+1}\mid y_{1:n})\). The predictive is visibly wider than any single red curve — it inherits both the spread of \(P(y_{n+1}\mid\theta)\) and the spread of the \(\theta_j\).
  • Figure 8.3 — Six Beta priors spanning flat, U-shaped, and concentrated shapes. Beta(1,1) is uniform; Beta(0.5,0.5) is U-shaped, favouring \(\theta\) near the boundaries; Beta(2,5) and Beta(30,10) are increasingly concentrated, at \(\theta\approx2/7\) and \(\theta\approx0.75\) respectively.
  • Figure 8.4 — Prior Beta(2,2) (dashed) and posteriors after \(n=10,50,200\) observations with \(\bar y=0.7\) held fixed. The posterior narrows at rate \(1/\sqrt n\) and moves onto the MLE as \(n\) grows, exactly the \(w\to0\) argument above made visible.
  • Figure 8.5 — Posterior predictive \(P(y_{n+1}\mid\mathbf y)\) (Negative Binomial, red) against a plug-in Poisson at the posterior mean rate (gray). Same mean, but the predictive has heavier tails: the two bars noticeably diverge above \(y_{n+1}=7\), and \(V(y_{n+1}\mid\mathbf y)\) exceeds the plug-in variance by the factor \(1+1/(\beta+n)=1.111\) derived above.
  • Figure 8.6 — Posterior predictive \(N(\mu_n, \sigma^2(1+1/\kappa_n))\) (red, solid) against a plug-in \(N(\mu_n,\sigma^2)\) at the posterior mean (gray, dashed). Same mean, but the predictive is visibly wider — the extra spread is entirely the remaining posterior uncertainty about \(\mu\).

Numerical Quadrature

  • Figure 9.1 — Equally spaced points in \(y\) (red ticks, right panel) map to unequally spaced points in \(x\) (red ticks, middle panel). The two shaded areas are equal: the transform changes where we look, not the value of the integral.

  • Figure 9.3 — The same likelihood (red) combined with three priors (blue). \(P(y)\) is the area under \(L(\theta)\pi(\theta)\) (shaded), computed here by integrate for a single parameter as a sanity check.
  • Figure 9.4 — Left: the posterior \(L(\mu,w)\pi(\mu,w)\) on its natural scale. Right: the transformed integrand \(g(u,v)\) on the unit square, with the midpoint grid at \(n=20\) overlaid. Equal probability mass under the two surfaces corresponds to equal volume, even though the shapes differ.
  • Figure 9.5 — Left: a correlated 2-D posterior inside a 12x12 grid; only the highlighted points carry non-negligible mass. Right: evaluations needed for a 20-point-per-axis grid as dimension increases.
